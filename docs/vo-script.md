# MOONSHOT — voice-over script

**One voice, 30 lines, about 235 words across three minutes.** That is deliberately
sparse. The film's argument is carried by the music and by a number on screen that
moves with real crowd telemetry; the voice is the third element and it works by
being intermittent. There are long stretches with nothing, and those are written
in, not gaps waiting to be filled.

---

## What the film is arguing about

Worth two minutes of your time before the lines, because the whole performance
turns on it.

The moon has been filed as finished. We went, we planted a flag, the item was
closed — and a completed world does not need power, so consumption has been capped
at what a completed world costs. That is the authority's position and it is not
about safety. It is accounting against a closed ledger.

The scientists in this film lost their allocation. They cannot run a laboratory on
nothing, so they built the apparatus out of people instead: light, sound, and a
roomful of attention pointed the same way. The number on screen is not a score of
how much fun they are having. It is signal strength, and it is the only evidence
that an unmetered supply exists — which is why he has to delete it rather than
merely disagree with it.

Then they aim it at the moon, because a closed file that stays visible keeps
getting reopened.

## Who is speaking

The antagonist. He is the author of *Defanged Noumena: Selected Wellness Writings,
1987–2007* — but he did not originate the argument so much as write it down for
it. That distinction matters to the performance: he is a mouthpiece who believes
he is an author. He is not the field staff, never attends the party he is closing,
and never touches a sound meter. He appears on screen four times, always in an
institutional corridor, always alone.

He is narrating in retrospect, to someone he assumes is sympathetic. He believes he
is the reasonable party in this film, and for most of its length the audience
should half-believe it too.

## The register — the most important note on this page

**Smug, not jackbooted.** He is warm, unhurried, faintly amused, and completely
certain. He is never angry, never raises his voice, never sneers, and never sounds
like a villain. Think of a senior public-health official who is genuinely pleased
to explain something to you, and who has never once been contradicted by anyone
whose opinion he valued.

The film only works if his case lands. His argument is that the great thing was
already achieved, that nothing outstanding remains, and that people still spending
themselves on aspiration are running a redundant process. That is a real argument
and a lot of people quietly hold it. Play it as one.

Two failure modes to avoid:

- **Menace.** No gravel, no whisper, no threat. The moment he sounds dangerous, the
  audience stops considering what he's saying and starts waiting for him to lose.
- **Camp.** No relish, no theatrical villainy. He is not enjoying his own wickedness
  because he does not think he is wicked.

The one place the mask slips is line 22, and it slips by about a centimetre. See
the note there.

## The one thing you are not being asked to play

You are the only voice in the film, but you are not the only party in it. There are
three. There is the thing he speaks for, which feeds on agreement and would rather
be conceded to than feared — that is why warmth is not a choice here, it is the
character's actual mechanism. There is the thing the crowd is assembling, which is
never named and never speaks. And there is the instrument: the readout that sits in
the corner of every frame of this film, reads for four acts, quietly declines to
back him up at 1:49, and takes a side at 2:46.

None of that needs to be in the voice. It is here so that you know what the silences
are doing, because several of them are another character's lines.

## Delivery

Conversational pace, slower than broadcast. Close, quiet, almost confiding — the
kind of level you'd use across a desk rather than into a room. Full stops are real
stops; several lines are two or three short sentences and the pauses between them
are doing work.

Accent: any educated register that reads as institutional rather than regional.
The reference is a policy paper read aloud by the person who wrote it.

---

## Recording spec

- **48 kHz, 24-bit WAV.** Mono.
- **Dry.** No compression, no EQ, no reverb, no de-essing, no noise reduction. The
  film's audio is synthesised from telemetry and the voice gets placed inside it;
  anything baked in fights that.
- **One file per line**, named by the line number in this script — `vo_07.wav` and
  so on. Do not assemble them into a single pass. Timings will move as the missing
  shots come in, and loose lines can be re-placed where a single take cannot.
- **Lines 28, 29 and 30 are additions and they are numbered out of order on purpose.**
  They belong at 0:36, 1:26 and 0:28 respectively and you will meet them in those
  places in the tables below. Numbering stayed put so that anything already recorded
  or discussed against the old numbers still matches. Read them in whatever order
  suits you; just name the files by the number, not the position.
- **Three reads of every line**, all in the same file, a couple of seconds apart:
  1. as written
  2. warmer — more genuinely kind
  3. drier — flatter, more administrative
- Leave a second of room tone at the head and tail of each file.
- **Thirty seconds of clean room tone** as its own file, please. It gets used under
  the silences.

Durations below are estimates at an unhurried pace. Don't perform to them — if a
line wants to be slower, take it slower and we will make room. Two exceptions, both
of which have to sit inside a fixed card: line 22, and line 27, which is the last
line in the film and has four seconds.

## One thing that is not in the script

There is no second voice and no counter-narration. Nothing rebuts him in words.

There is a third party — the instrument — and at 2:46 it takes the other side, but
it does so without speaking, because a signature is not an argument. Its refusal at
1:49 and its commitment at 2:46 are both silent, and both are stronger for it. The
rest of the rebuttal is the number on screen, which is real telemetry recorded off
a live crowd, and which climbs at the exact moment he declares there is nothing
left.

That was the whole reason to give the film to him to narrate. Please resist any
note that asks for a sympathetic narrator to argue back — the crowd already does
it, and it does it in data.
