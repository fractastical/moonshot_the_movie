# MOONSHOT — voice-over script

**Two voices, 36 lines, about 300 words across three minutes.** One voice is
speaking from afterwards, calm, certain it settled the matter. The other is
speaking from inside it, in the present tense, and does not yet know what is
about to happen to her.

That gap is the film's tension. Everything below is built to protect it.

---

## The story in plain words

Every spoken line and every word on screen is written to be followed by a
ten-year-old on one viewing, with no explaining afterwards. The whole plot is
this, and nothing in the film is allowed to be harder than this paragraph:

> People in charge said we already did the big thing — we went to the moon, so
> there is nothing important left to do. Because nothing is left to do, they cut
> everybody's electricity down to almost nothing. Some scientists lost their
> power and their money, so they cannot run their machines any more. They build a
> new machine out of the only thing that is still free: people. Three hundred
> people breathing, moving and dancing together in one room make a signal, and
> they point it at the moon. A number on the wall tells them how strong the
> signal is. It has to stay above eighty. It climbs, and it starts changing the
> people — they turn into beasts. Then a man from the government arrives with a
> meter. He does not stop the party. He pushes the number down, and when it
> climbs back anyway, he cuts the power. The number falls to zero. But the people
> keep dancing in the dark, because dancing does not need electricity. The number
> comes back, higher than it has ever been, and something on the other end wakes
> up and answers. Even the measuring machine changes sides. The man says there is
> nothing left. He is wrong, and his own screen says so.

Three checks are enforced by `tools/video/reading_level.py`, which reads the
lines out of this file and the on-screen words out of the panel renderer:
spoken lines and screen text must both score at or below fifth-grade
Flesch-Kincaid, and no line may collide with the next.

The rest of this document is direction, for the booth. It is written for adults
and is allowed to be.

## What the film is arguing about

**The moon was filed as finished.** We went, we planted a flag, the item was
closed. And a completed world does not need power, so consumption is capped at
what a completed world costs. That is the authority's position and it is not
about safety. It is accounting against a closed ledger.

**The condition has an official name and a real one.** Officially, *nothing left
to do* — no goals pending, so no spending warranted, and those exact four words
are on the console at 0:19 as B says them. The people living inside it call it
the Nothing, and it spreads: funding, then grids, then the sense that anything is
worth starting.

**The scientists lost their money and then their power.** You cannot run a
laboratory on nothing. So they are building the instrument out of the only
material nobody is charging for: people. Breath, light, sound, a few hundred
nervous systems in phase.

**They are aiming it at the moon** — not as a symbol, as a mass. It still moves
an ocean twice a day for free, which makes it the largest unmetered force
anybody can point at. The bet is that a crowd in phase can pull on it, and that
the pull is enough to wake something on the other end: an intelligence, or a
bond between one and the people who called it. Something that takes a side.
That is the moonshot. If it lands, the Nothing runs backwards.

## The number is the clock

This is the most important mechanical note in the document and it is new.

There is a readout in the corner of every frame of this film. It is real
telemetry, recorded off a live crowd, and it has never been touched. **B tells
the audience at 0:54 that it has to hold above eighty.** From that moment the
audience can read the film's outcome directly off the screen, and the recording
happens to contain a perfect three-act shape:

| when | it reads | what that is |
|---|---|---|
| 1:26 | **79.9** | The best they ever manage before the attack. One tenth short, and she says the number out loud. |
| 1:49 | 17.3 | He has forced it down. |
| 1:59 | 52.6 | **It is climbing back** while they dance in the dark — which is why he escalates to cutting the power. |
| 2:15 | **0.0** | Ten seconds at absolute zero. |
| 2:24.4 | **80.1** | It crosses eighty for the first time in the film, on the exact frame Act V begins. |
| 2:49 | **87.0** | Peak. Higher than anything before the attack. |

So they fail by a tenth, get erased, and then clear the bar they never reached.
None of that is invented; it is what the crowd did. Every number B says is the
number on screen at that instant — please do not paraphrase them, and if a line
moves in the edit we will re-check the value rather than change the word.

## Who is speaking, and in which tense

**A — THE AUTHOR. Past tense, always.** The antagonist, author of *Defanged
Noumena: Selected Wellness Writings, 1987–2007* — though he did not originate
the argument so much as write it down for it. A mouthpiece who believes he is an
author. He is narrating **after everything, to someone he assumes is
sympathetic**, and he thinks the file is closed. "We made a decision." "So we
removed the supply."

**B — THE SCIENTIST. Present tense, always.** She was in the lab when the power
went and she is in the room while it happens. She is **not** recalling this. She
is inside it and she does not know how it ends. "There is no power." "It is
coming back. Do not stop."

**Neither of you may break tense.** It is the entire suspense mechanism: a man
calmly explaining how he shut something down, laid over a woman who has not yet
lost. The audience works out from his tense alone that she is going to, and then
spends two minutes watching her not know. If she slips into past tense the film
becomes two people reminiscing and the tension evaporates.

**One deliberate exception, and it is an arc rather than a slip.** B is locked
in the present until B18 — "Zero. We think we killed it." — which is the last
moment she is inside the crisis. From B19 onward she is allowed to look back at
it, because by then it is survivable. Her tense loosening is the audience's cue
that the danger has passed, roughly fifteen seconds before the picture confirms
it. B3 also reports two recent events in the past; that is a person describing
what just happened to her, not narration from afterwards, and it should be read
at the same live pitch as everything around it.

They never address each other, never overlap, and she never learns he exists.

## The two registers

**A is smug, not jackbooted.** Warm, unhurried, faintly amused, completely
certain. Never angry, never raises his voice, never sneers. A senior
public-health official who is pleased to explain something to you and has never
been contradicted by anyone whose opinion he valued. The film only works if his
case lands — that the great thing was achieved, that nothing is left to do, and
that people still spending themselves on wanting things are running a redundant
process. Play it as a real argument.

His failure modes: **menace** (the moment he sounds dangerous the audience stops
considering him and waits for him to lose) and **camp** (he does not think he is
wicked, so there is nothing to relish). The mask slips once, at A10.

**B is an engineer under time pressure.** Not a mystic — never a mystic. The
ritual is engineering done with the only materials left and she describes it the
way you would describe a rig you built yourself. Precise nouns, flat delivery,
no awe. What makes her gripping is that she is doing the most important thing of
her life and refusing to editorialise about it.

Her failure modes: **wonder** (any breathiness turns the film into a fantasy and
hands him the argument) and **defiance** (she is not making a speech at him; she
does not know he is in this film). When she urges the room on at B17 she is
urging *the room*, not answering *him*.

## How the build works

**Act I — the theft.** He explains why the quiet is good and, in A2, states the
whole rule the film turns on: we already did the big thing, so nobody needs much
power. He claims authorship of that rule, and she answers it immediately with
what it cost her. Her last line reframes the moon from a closed file into a free
power source. That is the hook.

**Act II — the plan and the bar.** She states the whole thing in thirty seconds
and finishes by naming the number they have to beat. From here the audience can
score the film themselves.

**Act III — it works, and he arrives.** It climbs. It changes them. Then she
calls **79.9** — short — and three seconds later notices someone at the door
with a meter. That is the first moment the two halves of the film touch.

**Act IV — the squeeze, and it is aimed at the evidence.** He does not stop the
party, he stops the number. When it climbs back anyway he cuts the power. Her
line at 1:59 is her celebrating a recovery roughly one second before it dies.

**Act V — the answer.** He declares there is nothing left on the exact frame the
number crosses eighty for the first time in the film. She gets the last word.

His line at 1:36 — *"no one could tell me what it was for"* — is the hinge. The
audience has known since 0:54. It plays as a man who was told and did not
listen.

## The third party

Three parties, not two. The thing he speaks for feeds on agreement and would
rather be conceded to than feared, which is why his warmth is the character's
mechanism rather than a stylistic choice. The thing the crowd is assembling is
what B is reaching for. And the machine — the readout itself — reads for four
acts, refuses to back him up at 1:49, and picks a side at 2:46. It never speaks.
Several of the silences below are its lines. On screen it is always "the
machine", never "the instrument", and the actors should say it that way too.

## Recording spec

**Two actors, recorded separately.** They never overlap and never address each
other, so there is no need to get them in a room together.

- **Every line has been rewritten into plainer words since the last draft**, and
  a few have been re-timed or re-ordered with them. The characters, registers and
  tenses are unchanged, so prepared work carries over, but read from this version:
  the words differ in almost every line and several now name things the earlier
  draft only implied.
- **48 kHz, 24-bit WAV.** Mono.
- **Dry.** No compression, EQ, reverb, de-essing or noise reduction. The film's
  audio is synthesised from telemetry and the voice is placed inside it;
  anything baked in fights that.
- **One file per line**, named by line ID — `vo_A07.wav`, `vo_B13.wav`. Do not
  assemble them into a single pass.
- **Three reads of every line**, in one file, a couple of seconds apart:
  1. as written
  2. warmer — more genuinely kind (A) / more openly urgent (B)
  3. drier — flatter, more administrative (both)
- **B's numbers get a fourth read**: completely flat, as though reading a meter
  to a colleague rather than to us. We may prefer it.
- A second of room tone at head and tail of every file, and **thirty seconds of
  clean room tone per actor** as its own file.

Durations are estimates and every line has been checked against the picture for
room. Don't perform to them. The exceptions are lines that sit inside a fixed
card: A9, A14 and B22.

## What is deliberately not in this script

Nobody wins an argument out loud. A and B never address each other and never
share a line. They are describing the same events to different listeners, in
different tenses, and the audience is the only party hearing both.

Nothing rebuts him in words at the end either. The rebuttal is the number, which
is real, which he cannot edit, and which crosses her threshold at the exact
moment he says there is nothing left. Please resist any note asking B to answer
him directly. She never knows he is there, and that is why she is credible.
