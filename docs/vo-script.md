# TURN DOWN THE MOON — voice-over script

**One voice, 28 lines, about 215 words across three minutes.** That is deliberately
sparse. The film's argument is carried by the music and by a number on screen that
moves with real crowd telemetry; the voice is the third element and it works by
being intermittent. There are long stretches with nothing, and those are written
in, not gaps waiting to be filled.

---

## Who is speaking

The antagonist. He is the author of *Defanged Noumena: Selected Wellness Writings,
1987–2007* — the man who wrote the argument for turning celebration down to a safe
level. He is not the field staff. He never attends the party he is closing and
never touches a sound meter. He appears on screen four times, always in an
institutional corridor, always alone.

He is narrating in retrospect, to someone he assumes is sympathetic. He believes he
is the reasonable party in this film, and for most of its length the audience
should half-believe it too.

## The register — the most important note on this page

**Smug, not jackbooted.** He is warm, unhurried, faintly amused, and completely
certain. He is never angry, never raises his voice, never sneers, and never sounds
like a villain. Think of a senior public-health official who is genuinely pleased
to explain something to you, and who has never once been contradicted by anyone
whose opinion he valued.

The film only works if his case lands. His argument is that these people turned joy
into a metric — that they are scoring their own ecstasy, and that the number is the
disease. That is a real argument. Play it as one.

Two failure modes to avoid:

- **Menace.** No gravel, no whisper, no threat. The moment he sounds dangerous, the
  audience stops considering what he's saying and starts waiting for him to lose.
- **Camp.** No relish, no theatrical villainy. He is not enjoying his own wickedness
  because he does not think he is wicked.

The one place the mask slips is line 23, and it slips by about a centimetre. See
the note there.

## Delivery

Conversational pace, slower than broadcast. Close, quiet, almost confiding — the
kind of level you'd use across a desk rather than into a room. Full stops are real
stops; several lines are two or three short sentences and the pauses between them
are doing work.

Accent: any educated register that reads as institutional rather than regional.
The reference is a policy paper read aloud by the person who wrote it.

---

## Recording spec

- **48 kHz, 24-bit WAV.** Mono.
- **Dry.** No compression, no EQ, no reverb, no de-essing, no noise reduction. The
  film's audio is synthesised from telemetry and the voice gets placed inside it;
  anything baked in fights that.
- **One file per line**, named by the line number in this script — `vo_07.wav` and
  so on. Do not assemble them into a single pass. Timings will move as the missing
  shots come in, and loose lines can be re-placed where a single take cannot.
- **Three reads of every line**, all in the same file, a couple of seconds apart:
  1. as written
  2. warmer — more genuinely kind
  3. drier — flatter, more administrative
- Leave a second of room tone at the head and tail of each file.
- **Thirty seconds of clean room tone** as its own file, please. It gets used under
  the silences.

Durations below are estimates at an unhurried pace. Don't perform to them — if a
line wants to be slower, take it slower and we will make room. The one exception is
line 22, which has to sit inside a specific three-second card.

## One thing that is not in the script

There is no second voice and no counter-narration. Nothing rebuts him in words.
The rebuttal is the number on screen, which is real telemetry recorded off a live
crowd, and which climbs at the exact moment he declares there is nothing left. That
was the whole reason to give the film to him to narrate. Please resist any note
that asks for a sympathetic narrator to argue back — the crowd already does it, and
it does it in data.
